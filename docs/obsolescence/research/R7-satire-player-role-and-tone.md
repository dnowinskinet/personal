# R7-satire-player-role-and-tone.md

## Executive verdict

The strongest, most durable version of **Obsolescence, Inc.** is **not** a one-note satire about products “designed to fail.” It is a satire of **institutional incentive systems that make replacement, lock-in, managed inconvenience, and deferred consequences feel normal, rational, even customer-friendly**. The project brief already points in this direction: the game is about repeated launches, visible architectural contrast, and humor emerging from rational optimization rather than authored scolding. fileciteturn2file0 fileciteturn2file4

**Approved one-sentence satirical thesis:**  
**Obsolescence, Inc. satirizes the modern consumer-products corporation as a machine that turns sensible local decisions about segmentation, speed, elegance, support, compatibility, and growth into a system where replacement feels inevitable, responsibility is diffused, and the showroom stays beautiful while the costs accumulate somewhere else.** This thesis is better grounded than a simple “planned obsolescence is evil” framing because real-world evidence shows the problem is distributed across repair restrictions, support sunsets, subscriptions, cloud dependencies, trade-in funnels, ecosystem lock-in, and sustainability theater rather than only a single “bad part” hidden in a device. citeturn30search7turn30search1turn31search1turn36search0turn32search0turn26search3turn25search1

**The game’s argument:**  
The company’s real product is not just hardware. It is a managed cycle of novelty, dependency, and deferral. Devices are segmented into product lines to capture willingness to pay; software support becomes a timed promise; services turn ownership into conditional access; trade-ins convert last year’s installed base into this year’s demand; repair and refurbishment are tolerated when they protect margin and image, then constrained when they threaten upgrade velocity. The player should feel clever, not wicked, because each compromising decision solves a legitimate problem. Satire appears when those reasonable decisions pile up into absurdly visible outcomes: a gleaming launch event above ground, a repair floor choking below it, and a sustainability report that is technically true while the loading dock tells a fuller story. That argument fits the current flagship-building concept exceptionally well. citeturn23search1turn24search17turn30search7turn30search6turn31search1turn32search0turn32search2turn36search0turn25search1turn26search3

**Recommended player-role statement:**  
**The player is the company’s operating logic: an ongoing executive committee embodied as the institution itself, not a heroic founder and not a single department head.** That framing best justifies authority across design, suppliers, manufacturing, showroom, software, support, repair, trade-in, and disposal, while also making complicity structural rather than personal. It avoids turning the game into founder worship, but it still permits named executives, department heads, and era-specific personalities to pressure the player from inside the machine. This is also the role model most compatible with the brief’s desire to make the player responsible for attractive local optimizations inside a larger apparatus. fileciteturn2file7 fileciteturn2file12

**Target hierarchy:**  
The **primary targets** should be incentive structures, portfolio logic, executive euphemism, lifecycle deferral, lock-in strategy, sustainability theater, and the player’s own optimizing instinct. **Secondary targets** can include consumer aspiration, industry rituals, market-status signaling, and regulatory theater when treated as system pressures rather than evidence that ordinary people are foolish. **Forbidden or distracting targets** are ordinary workers, repair technicians, low-income customers, harmed communities, accessibility needs, and any one current company or executive rendered so specifically that the fiction collapses into topical parody. That hierarchy aligns with current regulatory, repair, and durability debates, which are focused far more on market structure and manufacturer practices than on blaming users or frontline labor. citeturn30search7turn30search6turn30search1turn30search8turn26search3turn25search1

**Verdict on title and concept:**  
**Retain _Obsolescence, Inc._ as the title, but broaden the public-facing thesis beneath it.** “Obsolescence” is still the best single word for the game’s visible outcome, but the playable thesis should clearly encompass **manufactured desire, ecosystem power, support sunsets, refurbishment, and waste**. In other words: keep the title, expand the argument. A broader title would be less sharp; a narrower design would be less durable. fileciteturn2file9 citeturn31search1turn31search8turn36search0turn32search0turn25search1

## Thematic options and evidence base

### What the real-world record supports

The documented record is unusually well suited to a systems satire. The FTC’s 2021 report to Congress said there was “scant evidence” for manufacturers’ standard justifications for many repair restrictions, and the agency later sued Deere, alleging that software and tool restrictions raised repair costs and reduced user autonomy. In Europe, the Repair Directive was adopted in 2024, and separate ecodesign rules for smartphones and tablets began applying in 2025 with requirements around spare parts, battery durability, repairability, and years of OS updates. The EU’s common-charger rules also came into force for many devices in late 2024, explicitly targeting convenience and e-waste. Those are not fringe complaints; they are regulatory responses to recurring market patterns. citeturn30search7turn30search6turn30search1turn30search8turn31search1turn31search8

The same is true for software and service lifecycles. Google’s Stadia shutdown announcement, Microsoft’s Windows 10 end-of-support pages, Google’s published Pixel update windows, Apple’s obsolete-products service policy, and Humane’s AI Pin shutdown all illustrate a world in which the meaningful life of a product is increasingly governed by support commitments, cloud services, and platform strategy as much as by hardware durability. That is precisely why the game should treat “how long we support it” as a major design lever rather than a background stat. citeturn36search0turn22search0turn22search2turn21search0turn34search0

The lifecycle evidence is equally strong. The UN’s Global E-waste Monitor reported 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022 and found that only 22.3% was formally collected and recycled, while the Basel Convention has tightened controls on both hazardous and non-hazardous e-waste movements as of January 2025. At the same time, manufacturers actively market trade-ins and certified refurbished products, which means the same firm can present itself as circular while also accelerating replacement cycles. That contradiction is exactly the sort of thing the game can stage visually without becoming a sermon. citeturn7search0turn25search1turn32search0turn32search2turn32search13

The consumer side is also more nuanced than a moralistic “people are dupes” thesis. Vance Packard’s classic distinction between functional obsolescence and “psychological” obsolescence still maps onto contemporary research, and recent studies suggest that perceived obsolescence, status concerns, convenience, and risk perceptions all shape replacement and repair decisions. One 2023 study found a paradox in which ecologically aware consumers may still replace products quickly when they perceive them as obsolete, while repair research shows willingness to pay for repair declining over time. That supports a satire aimed at aspiration and friction, not contempt for buyers. citeturn11search11turn33search2turn33search3turn12search11turn33search1turn33search21

### What the game comparables teach

The most useful game comparables converge on one lesson: the player should occupy an **institutional role with constrained empathy**, not a neutral spreadsheet god and not a powerless spectator. *Papers, Please* makes the player an immigration inspector whose salary and family needs reward speed and compliance; Lucas Pope’s official materials and dev notes explicitly frame document processing, end-of-day tallies, and partial consequence visibility as the structure that makes the player rationalize harm. *The Republia Times* simplifies this into editorial manipulation under authoritarian pressure. Both are strong models for complicity, but *The Republia Times* is thinner and more deterministic, while *Papers, Please* earns its force through repeated procedural labor. citeturn19search0turn19search6turn19search12turn18search7

*Not For Broadcast* is valuable because it makes manipulation playful. The player is a janitor forced into a live control booth, and the game turns censorship, ad selection, and public perception into tactile work. The lesson for *Obsolescence, Inc.* is that ideology lands better when filtered through a job the player must actually perform. *Frostpunk* shows the opposite tonal edge: it excels at necessity-driven moral tradeoffs and citywide consequences, but its severity often suppresses comedy. *Obsolescence, Inc.* should borrow Frostpunk’s causal clarity, not its constant grim grandeur. citeturn13search1turn13search5turn15search9

*Two Point Hospital* and *Prison Architect* are the most useful warnings about tone. *Two Point Hospital* shows how absurd art direction and warm character animation can keep management satire buoyant even when the institution itself is ridiculous. *Prison Architect* shows how easy it is for systems elegance to make real human suffering feel toyetic if the game under-emphasizes people as people. The design implication is clear: use human scale, queues, clutter, expressions, and downtime to preserve recognizability, but keep the joke aimed at policy, layout, throughput, and euphemism. citeturn18search1turn18search0turn18search12

*Cart Life*, *Big Pharma*, *Hollywood Animal*, *Good Company*, and *News Tower* fill out the rest of the lesson set. *Cart Life* demonstrates the power of mundane friction and small-scale survival pressures. *Big Pharma* proves that profit-versus-public-good satire can still be a satisfying production game, but also shows the risk of becoming too overtly thesis-first. *Hollywood Animal* is especially important because it gives the player multiple institutional fantasies, from boutique prestige to monstrous empire, instead of one “correct” path. *Good Company* and *News Tower* help with the flagship-building side: both make the organization itself visually legible and treat recurring public output as the backbone of play. citeturn15search1turn28search12turn29search11turn29search1turn28search14turn16search0

### Recommendation among the theses

The recommended thematic stack is:

**Primary thesis:** institutional incentives.  
**Structural frame:** lifecycle satire.  
**Major secondary theses:** manufactured desire and ecosystem power.  
**Supportive surface theme:** obsolescence.

That stack is the most mechanically fertile because it turns almost every gameplay layer into a source of satire. It also best matches the current flagship-building concept, where the showroom can dramatize desire and narrative while the underground floors dramatize throughput, support burden, waste, and deferred cost. Most importantly, it minimizes preachiness: the player is not asked to discover that “bad corporations do bad things”; the player is asked to keep a complicated machine running and then live inside its residue. fileciteturn2file10 citeturn31search1turn32search0turn36search0turn25search1turn26search3

## Player role, targets, and strategy model

### Recommended player role and player fantasy

A founder or CEO role is superficially attractive, but it makes the satire too biographical and too easy to misread as admiration for exceptional leadership. A head-of-product role is too narrow: it fits segmentation and roadmap decisions, but strains credibility once the player starts directing factory policy, trade-in operations, software sunsets, and disposal. A pure “corporation itself” abstraction solves scope, but risks thinning character writing and consequence. The best answer is a hybrid: **the player is the company’s operating system, personified through an executive committee that persists across eras**. This supports broad authority while preserving the sense that any one executive is partly trapped by the institution too. fileciteturn2file7 citeturn29search9turn16search0turn19search0

The fantasy should therefore be: **“Can I build a beautiful, admired, profitable product empire without becoming fully owned by its own incentives?”** That fantasy is stronger than “be a visionary inventor,” because invention alone cannot carry the game’s lifecycle or moral tension. It is also stronger than “be evil for profit,” because it preserves aspiration and a desire to keep building. The player should feel like a competent operator of a seductive machine, not a cartoon villain or a scolded student. fileciteturn2file14 citeturn28search14turn29search11turn15search9

### Primary, secondary, and forbidden targets

**Primary targets**

The primary objects of ridicule should be the firm’s own optimization logic: quarterly metrics, product-line segmentation, euphemistic executive language, lifecycle externalization, lock-in, compatibility breaks, support cost minimization, and the habit of relabeling extraction as convenience. These are the places where the documented record is richest and where the player’s own decisions can be made legible through systems rather than lectures. citeturn23search1turn24search17turn30search7turn31search8turn26search3turn36search0

**Secondary targets**

Secondary targets can include consumers’ attraction to novelty, review culture, launch ritual, “green” branding, and regulatory formalism. But these should be treated as pressures and performances, not as proof that everyone except the designer is stupid. The world should acknowledge that people often buy attractive, convenient, or status-laden products for understandable reasons. The joke is not “customers are sheep”; the joke is “the system learned how to monetize ordinary hopes.” citeturn11search11turn33search2turn32search0turn26search2

**Forbidden or distracting targets**

The game should not make workers, repair techs, support agents, low-income buyers, or affected communities into props for cleverness. Nor should it anchor itself to one recognizable present-day brand, connector, keynote format, or executive persona so tightly that the audience only sees topical parody. Composite practices are safer and also more truthful, because the evidence shows these dynamics recur across platforms, sectors, and regions. citeturn30search6turn10search14turn25search1turn31search1turn36search0

### Model for ethical, durable, and exploitative strategies

The most interesting strategic model is **not** “good is always correct,” and it is **not** “pure durability is structurally impossible from the first turn.” The sweet spot is this:

**Ethical durability is viable, but usually as a slower-growth path that becomes harder as scale, installed base, investor expectations, accessory revenue, and support burden accumulate.** In play terms, that means the durable path should work, but it should change the company you become. You can remain repair-friendly, transparent, and long-support, but you are likely to grow more slowly, accept fewer product tiers, spend more on service, and live with cannibalization of new launches by older products. Meanwhile, exploitative strategies should be powerful but not free: they create reputation debt, linewide liabilities, support cliffs, regulatory exposure, and organizational brittleness. That is far more replayable than either utopian ethics or deterministic doom. citeturn31search1turn30search1turn32search0turn32search2turn33search3turn7search0

Concretely, the game should support four recognizable but non-binary styles. A **durable specialist** can survive by accepting slower cadence, higher prices, and smaller audiences. A **balanced incumbent** can mix durability with selective lock-in and support discipline. An **aggressive growth company** can chase subscriptions, frequent refreshes, and accessory leverage. And a **predatory optimizer** can extract maximum short-term value while sowing future chaos. None should be fake. The durable specialist must be genuinely playable; the predatory optimizer must be genuinely tempting; the middle should be where most players drift. That mirrors the real market more closely than either purity test design or villain fantasy design. citeturn29search11turn28search12turn24search17turn37search1

## Complicity and mechanics-to-theme crosswalk

### How complicity should emerge

Complicity should emerge through **attractive local problem-solving**. The player is not tricked. The UI should tell the truth about the immediate upside: a single shared battery platform simplifies tooling and reduces cost; a proprietary connector sells accessories; a shorter software-support horizon saves support expense; a subscription stabilizes revenue; aggressive trade-ins clear inventory and stimulate demand; strict quotas raise throughput. The delayed consequence is where the satire lives, and it should be both visible and causally traceable. This structure reflects how real product systems work: line extension and versioning help capture demand; support and repair commitments impose long tails; cloud and subscription layers shift value away from the physical object; labor metrics and quality shortcuts can push risk downstream. citeturn23search1turn24search17turn30search7turn37search1turn10search14turn20search4

The player should therefore experience consequences in three beats. First comes **justification**: “this is sensible.” Then comes **residue**: clutter, queues, angry customers, internal memos, warranty claims, compliance notices, repair backlogs, obsolete stock, or strange revenue spikes. Finally comes **secondary adaptation**: the player must decide whether to paper over the problem, absorb the cost, or institutionalize a workaround. Satire is strongest when the workaround is itself funny and compromising. A charging-limit patch is funny not because software patches are a joke, but because a performance feature quietly becomes a battery-preservation feature in order to save a thin industrial design decision. fileciteturn2file5 citeturn31search1turn22search2turn20search4

The best organizing rule for all systems is:

**Every major mechanic should answer one of two questions: what story does the company tell, and where does the cost actually go?**

## Tone guide and subject matrix

### Tone pillars

**Deadpan competence**  
The funniest line is usually the calmest one. The world should describe harmful decisions in clean, operational language, because institutions make absurdity legible by refusing to call it absurd. This is closer to the FTC’s plain-language guidance style than to snarky parody. fileciteturn2file12 citeturn26search8

**Systems before punchlines**  
Humor should first appear in layout, throughput, queues, contradictions, and delayed consequences. Copy can sharpen the point, but it should not be responsible for creating the point from nothing. The building itself should do a large share of the comedic work. fileciteturn2file0 fileciteturn2file10

**Humanity at ground level**  
Workers, customers, and repair staff should speak in ordinary language about practical problems: pain, time, embarrassment, confusion, trust, rent, shift swaps, and broken devices. Executive language should be abstract, polished, and strategic. The tonal contrast between those registers is essential. citeturn10search14turn19search12turn15search1

**Bright surfaces, dark logistics**  
The visual world should remain attractive enough that players enjoy building and launching products. Darkness belongs in the consequences and backstage spaces, not in a uniformly grim aesthetic. *Two Point Hospital* is useful here: an institution can be silly and still recognizable. *Frostpunk* is the warning case: too much atmospheric gravity can crowd out playfulness. citeturn18search1turn15search9

**Sincerity at the point of harm**  
Once a subject crosses into injury, coercion, or clearly exploitative labor, the joke must travel upward toward policy and euphemism rather than downward toward people. Satire becomes stronger, not weaker, when the human cost is stated simply. citeturn20search4turn20search1turn10search14

### Permitted and forbidden comedic patterns

**Permitted patterns**

The game should permit comedy through contradiction, euphemism, visual overflow, warped KPI logic, cheerful launch choreography beside invisible backlog, polite letters announcing sweeping inconvenience, product names that oversell incremental changes, and quarter-end decisions that are defensible one department at a time but grotesque in aggregate. These patterns are truthful because real firms often present maintenance, lock-in, or reduced consumer choice as convenience or innovation. citeturn24search17turn26search8turn31search8turn37search1

**Forbidden patterns**

Do not use cruelty-as-surprise, contempt for poor or disabled customers, “idiot consumer” jokes, abuse of frontline workers as comic furniture, gleeful treatment of injury, or one-brand visual mimicry so precise that the fiction stops doing any work. Also avoid omniscient narrator sneering. The game should trust systems and contrast, not authorial superiority. fileciteturn2file14

### Voice and copy guide

**Executive language versus worker and customer language**

Executives should talk in abstractions: *portfolio coherence, support optimization, continuity strategy, post-purchase engagement, certified circularity, exception handling, compliance risk, harmonized accessories*. Workers and customers should use concrete nouns and verbs: *the bench is full, the battery swelled, the old charger doesn’t fit, three people came back angry, this patch made my screen slower*. This gap is one of the safest and funniest engines in the whole game. citeturn26search8turn30search7turn31search1

**How much real-world terminology to use**

Use real product and policy language **selectively**. Words like *warranty, unsupported, certified refurbished, update eligibility, genuine parts, trade-in credit, premium connectivity,* and *repairability* make the satire feel documented and current. But keep flagship terms fictional enough to avoid legal or topical drag. The user should feel “I have seen this,” not “this is obviously Brand X.” citeturn32search0turn32search2turn30search2turn37search1turn31search9

**Naming guidance**

Product names should be plausible first and silly second: *Nova*, *Nova Plus*, *Nova Studio*, *Nova Fold*, *HomeDock*, *Assist Band*, *AirLink*, *CircleCare*. **When to use absurdity, understatement, sincerity, and menace**

Use **absurdity** for product names, launch theatrics, internal KPIs, and visual contradictions. Use **understatement** for repair restrictions, support sunsets, and PR copy; that is where corporate language is funniest. Use **sincerity** whenever a worker, customer, or repair tech explains a concrete loss. Use **menace** sparsely, usually when a previously abstract tradeoff becomes an actual recall, injury risk, or labor crisis. That tonal ladder keeps the game charming while preserving seriousness where it matters. citeturn18search1turn15search9turn20search4

**Shared battery architecture** — Earlier decision: standardize on one glued battery layout across the whole lineup. Visible effect: repair benches fill with partly opened units while the showroom keeps selling their replacements. Responses: issue a charging-limit update, expand repair capacity, launch a trade-in drive, tighten definition of failure. ### Ending structures

**Lock-in empire ending**  
The player wins conventional success by building a beautifully integrated ecosystem with strong accessory revenue, subscriptions, support discipline, and high replacement velocity. The final irony is that the company becomes harder and harder to steer because its installed base, service obligations, and quarterly expectations now dictate future design. The ending should feel triumphant and claustrophobic at once. This is the satirical equivalent of “you succeeded so hard the machine owns you.” It fits the evidence on ecosystem services, subscriptions, and timed support windows. citeturn37search1turn22search0turn36search0

**Durable specialist ending**  
The player survives by becoming a smaller, admired, slower-growing company known for repairability, clear support promises, and restrained segmentation. Revenue is steadier, margins are thinner, product cadence is slower, and the building is visibly less chaotic below ground. This ending should not feel like a saint route; it should feel like a deliberate refusal of empire. The point is not “goodness wins,” but “harm mitigation is possible if you accept becoming a different kind of company.” This ending is supported by the existence of real durable-design movements and regulatory pushes, but it should remain difficult. citeturn30search1turn31search1turn33search21

**Breakup or interoperability ending**  
The company grows big enough, clever enough, or evasive enough that regulation catches up. A major ruling forces standardized charging, broader repair, or looser ecosystem control. The player may remain in charge, but not of the same machine. This ending externalizes the thesis: institutions do not always self-correct; sometimes they are corrected from outside. Use it sparingly so it feels like a campaign verdict, not a mandatory punishment. citeturn31search8turn30search8turn30search1

**Legacy burden ending**  
The company appears stable, but the true legacy is downstairs: warehouses of support obligations, unsold adapters, refurbishment queues, recall cages, and an installed base that will outlive executive plans. The company is still open. The launch event still runs. But the player’s last camera drift makes clear that the firm’s biggest product is now its own past. This ending best expresses the lifecycle thesis without a villain reveal. citeturn7search0turn25search1turn21search0

**Succession ending**  
A new leadership team inherits the machine. The player is shown how their own prior compromises have hardened into the new default options, tooltips, obligations, and blind spots. That is the cleanest proof that the satire is about institutions, not personalities. It also sets up replay elegantly. fileciteturn2file7

## Public presentation, title verdict, and revisit questions

### Public and LinkedIn-sharing risk assessment

This concept is **shareable in public and professional contexts** if it is framed as a systems satire about product lifecycle, incentives, and design tradeoffs rather than a topical takedown of one brand. In fact, the public-policy environment arguably makes it more shareable, not less: repairability, support longevity, green claims, and e-waste are now active mainstream regulatory topics in the US and EU, not niche anti-tech complaints. That means the premise can be presented as informed, current, and grounded in real debates over consumer rights and sustainable design. citeturn30search7turn30search1turn31search1turn26search3turn25search1

The main reputational risks are tonal. If the game becomes gleeful about injury, treats workers or poor customers as set dressing, or visually shadows one current company too closely, it will read as cheap or mean. If it overcorrects and becomes a vague civics lesson about capitalism, it will become bland and toothless. The safest public posture is: **playful, documented, humane, observant, and obviously composite**. The core talking point should be that the game is about how institutions create absurd outcomes from reasonable decisions, not about how consumers are fools or how one executive is evil. That framing is fully consistent with the evidence base and with the strongest game comparables. citeturn19search0turn13search1turn29search11turn28search12

### Title verdict

**Retain _Obsolescence, Inc._.**  
Do not rename the game around “desire,” “ecosystems,” or “the lifecycle,” even though those are important themes. “Obsolescence” has a sharper satirical silhouette and remains the best summary of what the machine produces across all the documented practices studied here. The adjustment should happen in the pitch line, not the title. Recommended public-facing elevator pitch:

**Obsolescence, Inc. is a management satire about building consumer-product empires whose most polished launches are only one brief moment in a lifecycle of segmentation, support promises, repair bottlenecks, upgrades, and waste.**

That version preserves the original bite while signaling the broader thesis. fileciteturn2file9 citeturn31search1turn36search0turn32search0turn25search1

### Questions to revisit after later research

Several tone and thesis questions should be revisited after R4 through R6 because mechanic detail will change the exact shape of complicity.

The first is **how much of repair and waste becomes physically simulated**. If repair is mostly abstract, the satire will need more help from copy and dashboards. If repair is spatial and visual, the game can rely much more heavily on visible contradiction and queue comedy. The brief already anticipates this linkage between tone and operations depth. fileciteturn2file10

The second is **how active old products remain during new launches**. The institutional-incentives thesis becomes stronger the more the installed base remains a live burden. If old products vanish into summary charts, the game will drift back toward launch-pageantry and away from lifecycle satire. That tradeoff belongs to campaign and long-term progression design as much as to writing. fileciteturn2file0

The third is **how the showroom reads customer differentiation**. If customer behavior becomes too abstract, the manufactured-desire and ecosystem-power themes will weaken. If customer AI becomes too individualized, the game may become sentimental and lose the clarity of institution-level satire. The target is legible group behavior with occasional vivid individuals, not thousands of tiny melodramas. fileciteturn2file15

The fourth is **how dark the game can become once real factory failure states exist**. Recalls, burnout, or hazardous defects are thematically valid, but the final tonal ceiling depends on how graphically and frequently those systems appear. This report recommends red lines, but the exact implementation should wait until the operations model is known. fileciteturn2file14

### Final recommendation

Proceed with **Obsolescence, Inc.** as an **institutional lifecycle satire** in which the player acts as the company’s ongoing operating logic. Treat **institutional incentives** as the primary satirical argument, **lifecycle visibility** as the structural frame, and **manufactured desire plus ecosystem power** as major secondary themes. Make the player complicit through comprehensible local optimizations. Keep the humor largely in systems, architecture, and deadpan euphemism. Preserve real humanity at the point of harm. Allow durable playstyles, but make them slower, harder, and structurally pressured rather than universally optimal. Build toward endings that reveal what the company’s true legacy is, without collapsing into a single “you were evil all along” twist.
